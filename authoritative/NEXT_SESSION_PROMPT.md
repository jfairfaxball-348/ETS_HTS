## Six directed exploration lanes (each should receive a genuine attempt)

### T — Time as a mathematical object
Investigate time not only as a real-number coordinate but as *clock readings, orderings, intervals, cycles or networks of observers*. Motivation: physical elapsed proper time depends on a worldline/metric and can differ with velocity and gravitational environment; coordinate time and human perception are distinct ideas. Inspect proper time in special/general relativity, Minkowski interval/causality, clock comparison and synchronisation, discrete clock graphs, event partial orders and periodic time. Extract elementary finite models (e.g. clocks linked by local comparisons, loops whose pairwise agreements may conflict, cyclic timelines, different-rate updates). Look for sharp necessity/sufficiency, reconstruction or obstruction questions. Do NOT claim that clocks move differently merely because "time is subjective"; declare the model and hypotheses.

### I — Infinity and finite-versus-infinite implications
Take the owner's intuition "if infinity exists, X; if not, Y" seriously as a request for **precise conditional mathematics**, not a premise about empirical existence. Distinguish unbounded natural numbers, infinite sets, actual/potential infinity, infinite sequences, and infinite limits. Specify the chosen axiom system (ordinary mathematics/ZFC, finite structures, or a named alternative) whenever the distinction matters; do not falsely assert that mathematics has settled metaphysical infinity or that the negation of infinity is a single consistent theory. Examine finite-to-infinite principles, infinite pigeonhole phenomena, compactness, König-style branching, eventual versus arbitrarily long finite behaviour, and statements whose finite versions break. Seek new *finite, school-readable* threshold questions motivated by infinity; record familiar facts and logical tautologies as kills.

### P — π, circumference, radius and changes of geometry
Begin with the precise Euclidean invariant circumference/diameter = π, rather than suggesting π itself randomly changes. Ask what happens when we keep the ideas of "circle", "distance", "perimeter" and "radius" but change the underlying rules: curved surfaces, grid/taxicab metrics, polygons, discrete lattices, higher dimensions, approximation or limiting curves. Are there unexpectedly universal inequalities, forbidden ratios, exact finite thresholds or reconstruction laws? Define the metric and measurement scheme; verify trivial scaling and known results before suggesting a conjecture. π should generate *mathematical surprise* rather than a numerology exercise.

### B — The mathematics of the brain / brainwaves
Use brain rhythms as inspiration for accessible mathematics of **coupled oscillators, phase, periodicity, interference, synchronisation, signals and networks**. Formulate precise discrete or continuous models: finite strings of pulses, graph-connected oscillators, frequency ratios, delays, threshold rules, phase-locking and changing dynamics. Explore what local timing constraints force globally and when different rhythms can coexist. Separate proved properties of a mathematical model from empirical neuroscience or medical assertions; do not claim an unmeasured brainwave mechanism, diagnose anyone, or invent EEG data. Consult actual accessible neuroscience/signal-processing literature and label its scope clearly. Prefer problems with a completely self-contained student-readable version.

### Z — Division by zero and the boundary of arithmetic
Treat 1/0 and 0/0 as undefined in ordinary real-number field arithmetic, not as a hidden real number. Investigate what axioms fail if division is totalised or a new infinity/error value is adjoined, and what consistent alternative structures allow (projective or extended number lines, wheels, meadows, partial operations, limits, removable singularities). Build concrete finite operation tables or simple algebraic rules and ask exact impossibility, uniqueness, minimisation or compatibility questions. Check elementary contradiction 0·x=0 and cancellation/distributivity first, and kill disguised textbook contradictions. Never advertise "division by zero solved" by silently dropping familiar rules.

### C — Convergence, divergence and rows of numbers
Examine sequences, infinite sums, recursive rows of numbers, triangular arrays, and double-indexed limits; make clear which object is intended. Start with striking examples: terms going to zero while sums diverge, alternating versus positive series, rearrangements, different row/column limiting orders, and local rules producing unpredictable long-run behaviour. Formulate finite-prefix-to-limit implications, sharp convergence tests for constrained digits/rows, and recurrence or ordering conditions. Name known results (geometric/harmonic/alternating series, comparison tests, Riemann rearrangement where relevant) and actively prevent rediscovering them as fresh open problems.
